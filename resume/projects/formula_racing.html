Formula SAE — Timing, Telemetry & Bodywork

A set of hardware and electronics projects built for our Formula SAE race car — a lap timing system, a custom vehicle telemetry logger, and hands-on composite bodywork — built because commercial equivalents were out of budget for a student team.

Overview

As part of our Formula SAE team, I worked across electrical, mechanical, and software subsystems to help get our competition car ready for annual SAE events. A recurring theme across that work was building things ourselves that a bigger program would just buy — commercial data acquisition systems were well outside a student team's budget, so several of my contributions were custom hardware and firmware built from scratch to fill that gap.

Lap Timing System

I designed and built a custom lap-timing system using an Arduino and garage-door sensors to detect vehicle pass events in real time, applying foundational electrical engineering principles to get reliable readings out of hardware that was never meant for this.

When the hardware display we'd planned to use turned out to be unreliable, I diagnosed the issue and implemented a fallback that routed serial output directly to a console instead, so we could keep monitoring lap times without losing data. The whole system went through several hardware revisions, refined through iterative testing with the rest of the team.

Vehicle Telemetry Data Logger

We needed a way to log real-time vehicle telemetry during testing runs, and since commercial data acquisition systems were out of budget, I built a custom data logger using an Arduino and C++ instead.

That meant interfacing multiple sensors, handling SPI bus communication to write data to an SD card reliably at high speeds, and writing firmware that could hold up under actual vehicle vibration without dropping packets or crashing mid run — the kind of reliability problem that only shows up once you're testing on a real car instead of a bench.

Key challenge: Getting high-speed SD card writes to hold up reliably under real vehicle vibration, without dropping telemetry packets or crashing the firmware mid-run.

Cornering Data Acquisition

I also built a data-logging system to capture cornering behavior — things like steering input and lateral load — which the team used to inform toe and camber adjustments for better handling.

Note: I'm still tightening the wording on exactly how this data was used (logged for post-run analysis vs. feeding into a live adjustment) — let me know and I'll lock this section in.

Composite Bodywork Fabrication

Outside the electronics, I also worked hands-on fabricating aerodynamic body panels using fiberglass cloth and resin layup techniques, balancing weight reduction against structural durability for competition use. That meant getting familiar with composite resin chemistry and curing processes well enough to meet both performance and safety requirements.

What I Learned

Working on the racing team meant constantly moving between disciplines — writing firmware one week, laying up fiberglass the next — and coordinating with teammates whose expertise didn't overlap much with mine. It's where I got most of my hands-on electrical engineering experience, and it taught me how to build something reliable under real deadline and budget pressure instead of ideal conditions.
